# The Thesis in Plain English

## What is happening?

Kazakhstan sells China mostly oil, metals and ores, and buys from China clothes, shoes, machinery, cars and electronics. Since 2013, when China launched the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) with a speech in Astana, three things changed. Kazakhstan used to sell China more than it bought; now it buys far more (by China's figures, a surplus of USD 3.5 billion in 2013 became a deficit of USD 12 billion in 2024). Oil went from most of what Kazakhstan sells China to about a fifth. And new products appeared, especially sunflower oil, animal feed, wheat and oilseeds.

## What question has not been answered?

People usually say the BRI caused these changes. But oil prices crashed in 2014–2016, and that alone changes what Kazakhstan sells to everyone, not just China. The studies reviewed for this thesis had not checked whether the changes in trade with China were *special to China* and *started with the BRI*, or whether they were the same changes visible in Kazakhstan's trade with the rest of the world, or trends that started earlier.

## Why does the BRI matter here?

The BRI is the obvious candidate explanation. After 2013 China financed roads and border posts in Kazakhstan, a big dry port opened at Khorgos on the border, and the two governments signed agreements letting Kazakh farm products into China. If the BRI mattered for trade, it should show up as China-specific changes that start after 2013.

## What does the research investigate?

It looks at each type of product Kazakhstan exports (97 product chapters) and compares, year by year, how much went to China with how much went to everyone else. If oil prices fall, they fall for every buyer; comparing China with the rest of the world cancels that out. What remains is what changed *specifically with China*. The research then checks *when* that change started.

## How does the method answer the question?

Think of it like this: if Kazakhstan's wheat exports to China rise by 50 per cent while its wheat exports to everyone else also rise by 50 per cent, nothing China-specific happened. If they rise by 50 per cent to China and stay flat elsewhere, something did. The statistical model does this comparison for every product and year at once. A second model asks whether the China-specific trend got faster after 2013, which is what you would expect if the BRI caused it.

## What do the findings establish?

1. **The trade balance got worse for two different reasons at two different times.** From 2013 to 2019 it was because oil sales to China collapsed. From 2019 to 2024 it was because imports from China doubled. Kazakhstan's mineral exports to China actually *grew*, so they are not the problem.
2. **The one clearly China-specific change is food and farm products.** Kazakhstan now sends about a fifth of its agri-food exports to China, up from almost nothing. But this started around 2010–2012, before the BRI, and its growth rate did not speed up after 2013.
3. **China's money went in before the BRI.** Chinese state lending to Kazakhstan was largest in 2008–2012, mostly for pipelines, oil companies and copper mines. After 2013 it was smaller and went to roads and border posts. None went to farming.
4. **Kazakhstan's own statistics have holes.** For 2020–2022 they leave out copper, zinc and aluminium exports and machinery imports. China's records fill the gap.

So the BRI is the right *background* for the story and fits the food-exports change, but there is no evidence that it *caused* the change in what Kazakhstan sells China.

## Why should anyone care?

Because policy is being made on the assumption that the BRI transformed this trade. If the balance worsened because of oil prices and an import boom, the response is different from what it would be if it worsened because of the BRI. If farm exports to China grew because of market-access deals and logistics rather than Chinese loans, that tells Kazakhstan where to focus its export promotion. And if the national statistics leave out large parts of trade with China in some years, analyses built on them alone will be wrong.

## One-paragraph version for the defence

"I asked whether the changes in Kazakhstan's trade with China after 2013 were really about China and the BRI, or just the oil price cycle. By comparing each product's exports to China with its exports to the rest of the world, I removed the price cycle. The balance with China worsened first because oil collapsed and then because imports doubled; mineral exports actually grew. The only big China-specific change is in food exports, and it started before the BRI and did not speed up afterwards. So the BRI is the context and fits the food story, but the data do not show that it caused the changes. Along the way I found that Kazakhstan's own statistics for 2020–2022 leave out copper and machinery, so China's data have to be used."
