# Pillar 1: Asymmetric Interdependence as the Primary Lens

The primary theoretical frame is asymmetric interdependence, not dependency theory. Keohane and Nye's interdependence program directs attention to the distribution of sensitivity and vulnerability across linked economies: trade ties can expand welfare while also creating leverage when one side can adjust more easily than the other [@keohane_nye_1973]. Hirschman's classic account of foreign trade as a source of national power makes the mechanism sharper: dependence is politically consequential when a partner can threaten exit or reorientation at lower cost than the dependent state [@hirschman_1945].

This lens fits Kazakhstan-China trade better than a simple core-periphery story. Kazakhstan is not a passive periphery: it has used China-facing infrastructure, finance, and commodity exports to diversify away from Russian transit channels and to monetize mineral endowments. Yet the structure of the relationship is asymmetric. China is a very large buyer, creditor, infrastructure builder, and technology supplier; Kazakhstan is a smaller economy with a concentrated export basket. The relevant hypothesis is therefore not that BRI mechanically subordinates Kazakhstan, but that post-2013 connectivity can deepen exposure to commodity-specific Chinese demand while shifting bargaining power toward the actor with more outside options.

Dependency theory remains useful as intellectual history because it foregrounds unequal exchange and the risk that infrastructure locks exporters into low value-added roles. It is not the lead theory here because it tends to overpredict structural subordination and understate agency, sectoral variation, and policy choice. The empirical strategy therefore asks whether the post-BRI period is associated with a change in the trade-balance return to mineral exports, not whether BRI caused dependency as a total social formation.

# Pillar 2: Gravity, Trade Costs, and BRI Trade Facilitation

The gravity tradition supplies the economic channel. Anderson and van Wincoop show that bilateral trade depends on trade costs relative to multilateral resistance, so the effect of a corridor depends not only on the Kazakhstan-China link but also on how the corridor changes alternative routes and partner options [@anderson_vanwincoop_2003]. BRI transport projects should matter most where they reduce shipment time, border delays, and logistics costs.

The BRI economics literature supports this channel but also cautions against treating infrastructure as a uniform shock. de Soyres and coauthors use GIS-based shipping-time estimates to show that BRI infrastructure can reduce trade costs, with the largest gains concentrated along corridors [@desoyres_etal_2019; @desoyres_etal_2020]. Baniya, Rocha, and Ruta estimate gravity models and find that BRI-related improvements can raise trade flows, especially when transport investment is complemented by trade reforms [@baniya_rocha_ruta_2020]. Bird, Lebrand, and Venables focus directly on Central Asia and show that spatial effects are uneven: some locations gain from new connectivity, while others face intensified competition or bypass effects [@bird_lebrand_venables_2020].

For this thesis, the implication is that a post-2013 dummy alone is too crude. A credible empirical design should combine a time-series test for structural change with covariates for commodity prices, exchange rates, GDP, and Chinese finance intensity. The ARDL specification does that within Kazakhstan's short annual sample. Synthetic control would be useful if donor bilateral series were available, but the current local data do not support it.

# Pillar 3: Resource Dependence, Terms of Trade, and Volatility

Kazakhstan's China-bound exports are resource-heavy, so BRI must be interpreted through resource-dependence and terms-of-trade channels. Sachs and Warner's resource-abundance argument links high primary-resource dependence to slower long-run diversification in cross-country data [@sachs_warner_1995]. van der Ploeg reviews the conditions under which resources become a curse or a blessing, emphasizing volatility, institutions, real exchange-rate pressures, and fiscal management [@vanderploeg_2011].

Commodity-price history matters because a rising mineral export series can reflect world prices rather than integration effects. Harvey, Kellard, Madsen, and Wohar show that long-run primary commodity prices do not move as a stable, monotonic gift to exporters; trend and break behavior differ across commodities and periods [@harvey_etal_2010]. The empirical strategy therefore controls for Brent and copper prices and treats mineral-export coefficients as conditional correlations. The missing uranium price proxy is a limitation, especially because uranium is part of the fixed HS-6 basket.

The central empirical question is whether post-BRI mineral exports are associated with a stronger or weaker trade-balance payoff after accounting for commodity prices and macro controls. A negative interaction would be consistent with a pattern in which higher mineral exports coincide with larger imports, financing outflows, or price-cycle exposure. It would not by itself prove exploitation or dependency.

# Pillar 4: Global Value Chains and Upgrading

The GVC literature clarifies what a positive BRI outcome would look like. Gereffi, Humphrey, and Sturgeon define governance types that shape whether firms and countries remain in captive supplier roles or move toward more complex capabilities [@gereffi_humphrey_sturgeon_2005]. Applied to Kazakhstan, the question is whether China-facing trade and finance support downstream processing, logistics services, and manufacturing linkages, or whether they reinforce raw-material exchange.

Recent Central Asia evidence is mixed. Jepson and Sweeney examine Chinese state capital and downstream linkages in Bolivia and Kazakhstan, directly addressing whether resource-based economies can use Chinese capital for structural transformation [@jepson_2024]. Calabrese's Kyrgyzstan study similarly treats BRI finance as a potential industrialization tool rather than only a debt or dependency mechanism [@calabrese_2024]. Petry shows that BRI is not only about roads and railways; financial infrastructures can reorganize capital access and market architecture, including in Kazakhstan-linked settings [@petry_2023].

The GVC pillar therefore turns the thesis away from a one-dimensional trade-volume question. If post-BRI mineral exports rise but the trade balance weakens or the mineral interaction turns negative, the result may point to limited upgrading rather than simply more trade. Conversely, if mineral exports and trade balance improve together, that would be consistent with a more favorable resource-based integration path.

# Kazakhstan and Central Asia BRI Evidence Since 2018

Kazakhstan-specific and Central Asia-specific studies show why aggregate BRI claims need local testing. Shah's Kazakhstan case study emphasizes that Sino-Central Asian engagement combines Beijing's regional strategy with local implementation and perception dynamics [@shah_2018]. Bird, Lebrand, and Venables model Central Asian geography and show that corridor benefits are spatially differentiated [@bird_lebrand_venables_2020]. Kahraman's spatial empirical analysis extends the regional evidence by testing economic performance patterns in Central Asia and the South Caucasus [@kahraman_2025].

Survey and field-based work also matters. Primiano and Kudebayeva find that Kazakhstan's reception of BRI is uneven rather than uniformly enthusiastic [@primiano_kudebayeva_2025]. Mostowlansky's Khorgos study shows how BRI soft power, border security, and loyalty suspicions interact at the Sino-Kazakh frontier [@mostowlansky_2020]. Brassett's work on Sino-Kazakh transboundary water management adds an environmental-governance channel that is not visible in trade data [@brassett_2023]. Together with Jepson and Sweeney, Calabrese, and Petry, these studies justify a cautious interpretation: BRI is a package of infrastructure, finance, border governance, and political-economic reorientation, not a single treatment with homogeneous effects.

# Synthesis for the Dissertation

The literature leads to three design choices. First, asymmetric interdependence is the lead theory because it can accommodate mutual gains and unequal bargaining at the same time. Second, gravity and trade-cost models justify testing for post-2013 trade-balance changes, but they also imply that infrastructure effects should be conditional on commodities, prices, and macro controls. Third, resource and GVC literatures make the mineral interaction central: the thesis should ask whether the China-facing mineral channel improves Kazakhstan's external position or deepens a commodity-concentrated pattern.

The empirical strategy should therefore be described as convergent rather than singly causal. ARDL and structural-break tests provide within-country time-series evidence. Synthetic control and partner-placebo DiD would provide stronger causal evidence, but the local data currently do not contain the donor or placebo panels required to estimate them. The results section must state this limitation prominently and avoid causal language beyond what the available designs support.
